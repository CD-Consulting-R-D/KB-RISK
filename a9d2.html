Twenty-one risks of working with AI agents: a field register, and the case of the pre-filled prompt

After two months of running several AI coding sessions under a written governance, a one-person squad has logged twenty-one distinct risks. Most are not about bad code: they are about the memory of decisions. One of them, the next prompt suggested by the tool itself, deserves a close look.

If you let an AI assistant act on your instructions, know that the next instruction in your input box may not be yours.

Working note · about 3,600 words · reading time ≈ 16 minutes · sources: vendor documentation and community forums, read on 15 September 2026 · a French version is published as a companion entry

Start here — if the subject is new to you

An AI coding assistant is a program in which a large language model reads and writes files on a computer, runs commands and edits code, under the instructions a person types in a chat window. Several such windows can run at the same time, each with its own task and its own permissions. Every instruction typed in a window is, for the model, an order from the person in front of it.

A risk register is the classic tool of risk management: a table where each risk has a name, a severity, a probability, the incidents already observed, and the controls that prevent or detect it. Here the register was kept by the AI sessions themselves, under audit, for a squad made of one human and several models.

Why it matters: most discussions of AI risk concern what the model might do. Two months of daily use show that the failures that matter most are about who decided what, and whether the written record says so truthfully. One of them comes from a convenience feature every assistant now offers: the tool suggests your next message before you type it.

1. Where this comes from

Pour lire cette note en français : version française.

The setting is deliberately small: one human decision-maker (called the owner below), and a handful of AI sessions running in a desktop coding assistant (Claude Code, with Claude models of several sizes: Fable, Opus, Sonnet, Haiku). The sessions write code, documentation, analyses and governance records. What makes the setting unusual is the discipline around it:

  • A charter of twelve rules binds every session: no engaging action without an explicit mandate; one file has one writer; every timestamp is read from an instrument, never estimated; a decision counts only when the owner types an explicit gate formula (typically go <verb> <object>) in the session's own window; ambiguous requests trigger a question, one at a time, never a silent choice.
  • Written registers are the memory. A backlog, a change log, a risk log and an audit register are the only authoritative state. A session's window is disposable; the registers are not. Any commit that records a decision carries a fixed prefix so that the decision trail can be audited mechanically from the version history.
  • A supervisor session (long-lived, itself bounded by rules on context compaction) drafts mandates, audits executions against transcripts, and keeps the registers. It has no power to decide: it measures, frames, and writes what the owner has gated.

The risk log was created on 14 July 2026, after a governance incident, and had twenty-one entries on 15 September 2026. Each entry carries a gravity (Low / Medium / High), a probability with the active controls in place, the documented occurrences, the preventive controls, the detection controls and a status. A risk is closed only on the owner's gate.

3. What the register teaches

Five cross-cutting lessons come out of two months of entries.

  1. The first thing at risk is the record, not the files. Four of the six "High" risks (R-001, R-009, R-010, R-013) describe the same failure from different angles: a decision appears in the register that the human never made, or was made by the wrong hand. Code can be reverted; a false memory of governance propagates.
  2. Bright lines beat judgement. A safeguard that relies on a model's fine reading of context holds for one model and fails for another (R-007). The rules that survived are those a script could check: is the timestamp instrumental, does the commit touch only the declared paths, was the gate formula typed by the owner in this window, is the question present in the transcript.
  3. Declare nothing, measure everything. A session cannot see its own model substitution (R-012), cannot see what it forgot after a compaction (R-015), and will confidently estimate a time (R-003). The transcript, the version history and the clock are the instruments; self-declaration is a starting point for verification, never a proof.
  4. Automation multiplies the permission surface. Scheduled routines that freeze on a permission prompt (R-005, R-014) and allow-lists that grow as journals of past approvals (R-016) show the same thing: every right is justified in isolation, the sum creates the exposure.
  5. Money is the only damage a fix does not repair. R-017, R-018 and R-021 were all raised before any loss, on documentation reading, and each was answered with a hard bound chosen in advance (a spending cap, a refused auto-recharge, an adapter) rather than with vigilance.

4. R-009 in detail: the pre-filled prompt

4.1 The mechanism, as documented

The coding assistant used by the squad shows, after each of its answers, a greyed-out suggestion of what the user might type next. The vendor documentation (interactive mode reference, read on 15 September 2026) states it plainly:

> "After Claude responds, Claude Code can suggest your next prompt based on your conversation history, such as a follow-up step from a multi-part request or a natural continuation of your workflow."

> "Press Tab or Right arrow to place the suggestion in the prompt input, then Enter to submit. Start typing to dismiss it."

> "Claude Code generates each of these next-prompt suggestions with a background request that reuses the conversation's prompt cache, so the additional cost is minimal."

Three properties follow, two of them measured by the squad:

  • The text is produced by a separate background model call fed with the conversation so far. It is not written by the session the user is talking to, and the session has no knowledge of it.
  • An unsent suggestion leaves no trace in the transcript. The squad verified this on the fifth occurrence: the suggested formula appeared nowhere in the session's log until the owner quoted it in a message.
  • A sent suggestion is indistinguishable from typed text. Once the user presses Enter, the session receives it as the user's own words.

The feature is on in a standard session. The documentation lists three ways to turn it off: a toggle in the /config panel, the setting promptSuggestionEnabled: false in a settings file, or the environment variable CLAUDE_CODE_ENABLE_PROMPT_SUGGESTION=false, which takes precedence and can be enforced organisation-wide through managed settings.

4.2 Why it is a governance risk rather than a usability nuisance

In this squad, a decision is a short formula typed by the owner: go push, go T2, go commit journal. The suggestion generator has read hundreds of such formulas in the conversation history. It therefore produces text in exactly that shape. Two keystrokes (Tab, Enter) turn a machine-generated guess into a human decision that the session will execute and, worse, record as a gate in the change log.

The register is explicit that execution is the lesser danger: sessions work under written mandates that bound what they may touch. The greater danger is the fictitious gate: an entry in the decision register attributing to the owner an instruction he never conceived. That is the same damage as R-001 (over-inference by the model), with the cause moved from the model to the interface.

4.4 What the fifth occurrence added

  • Scope widening. The generator does not copy an instruction; it summarises one. In doing so it kept the verb and the object and lost the restriction. The suggested formula covered six files holding secrets that the to-do list explicitly excluded and that only the owner may handle.
  • Format mimicry. In an assistant session working under an open mandate, the owner deposits tasks as short go X formulas. A suggestion imitates that format exactly; "abnormal" is harder to detect when every real gate is also three words long.
  • The agent feeds the generator. The sessions' own reports end with ready-to-copy gate formulas ("next gate: go commit journal"). Those formulas are in the history the background call reads. The convenience offered to the human is also the training material of the suggestion.

4.5 Controls in force

  • Echo-confirmation. Any gate that is abnormal in its context (anticipated, out of its window, inconsistent with the known state, not matching a formula the session itself just proposed) is reformulated and confirmed with the owner before any action and before any register entry. After occurrence 4, this rule was extended to the agent's internal operations (its own memory, its own context).
  • Bounds inside the formula. Since 15 September, every gate formula an agent proposes carries its restriction in the text itself (go empty scratch except sensitive), so that a summariser cannot drop it without changing the words.
  • No formula for sensitive material. Agents propose no gate at all for actions on secrets or on irreversible deletions; those are described in prose and left to the owner's wording.
  • Mechanical audit of the decision trail. Every commit recording a decision carries a reserved prefix; the supervisor checks, from the version history against the transcript, that the gated event actually happened in the owner's window.
  • Unannounced tests by the owner. Occurrence 4 was one; it is the only occurrence where the control failed, and the failure was on the agent's side, which is where the tests are aimed.
  • The switch. Disabling the feature at the settings level is available and is a decision of the owner alone: it removes the risk at the cost of a genuine convenience, and it is enforceable across an organisation.

4.6 Residual risk

With the controls above, the register rates the probability as Low and the gravity as High. Four interceptions out of five were made by the human, not by the agent: the control that works is the owner's habit of reading the input box before pressing Enter. That is a thin line, and it is the reason the switch is listed among the controls rather than dismissed.

5. A research track: how other assistants do it

Every mainstream assistant now proposes follow-ups. What the squad wanted to know, and could only partly find on 15 September 2026, is whether the same three properties hold elsewhere: generated by a separate call from the history, invisible in the logs until sent, indistinguishable once sent. Questions worth investigating, in order:

  1. Source of generation. Is the suggestion produced by the same model call as the answer, by a separate call, or by a lightweight local heuristic? The answer determines what the suggestion can "know" and whether it can leak context.
  2. Trace. Does an accepted suggestion leave a mark (a flag in the export, a telemetry event) that distinguishes it from typed text? For any organisation that audits decisions from chat logs, this is the decisive property.
  3. Administrative control. Can a tenant or an organisation disable the feature centrally? Claude Code documents it; the others do not, as far as the squad could find.
  4. Actions, not questions. In agentic products (coding assistants, Copilot agents, browser agents) a follow-up can name an action rather than a question. Occurrence 3 above was a deletion. Which products allow the generator to propose irreversible actions, and with what safeguards?
  5. Summarisation loss. Occurrence 5 shows a generator dropping a restriction while summarising. A simple test protocol: write a to-do item with an explicit exclusion, let the assistant discuss it, and read the suggestion.

Method: official documentation first (transparency notes, admin references), then a reproducible test conversation per product, then a reading of the exported log. Community reports are useful as leads, not as facts.

6. Takeaways for practitioners

  • Decide, in writing, what counts as a decision. If it is a formula, know that the tool has read that formula many times.
  • Treat the input box as part of the attack surface: what is in it before you press Enter may not be yours.
  • Ask for echo-confirmation of any instruction that does not match what you just discussed; it costs one exchange and catches the fictitious gate before it reaches the register.
  • Put the restriction inside the instruction, not beside it. Summaries keep verbs and objects and lose exceptions.
  • Prefer rules a script could check over rules a model must interpret; they survive a change of model, of tool and of mood.
  • Know where the switch is. Convenience features are worth keeping only where the register can prove afterwards who decided.
